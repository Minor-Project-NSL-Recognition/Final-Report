\chapter{Data Collection Tool}
\label{app:recorder}

Clips were recorded with a purpose-built tool that runs MediaPipe Holistic on a live webcam feed and saves the 225-value landmark vector for each frame. The operator selects a signer identity and a phrase, then starts and stops each recording explicitly. Landmark overlays are drawn live, so a failed detection is visible during recording instead of afterwards.

The first five pose landmarks of the clip's first frame, in the normalised image coordinates MediaPipe reports:

    \caption{Raw pose landmarks 0 to 4, first frame}

\noindent Both hand blocks of this frame are entirely zero. The signer's hands had not yet entered the frame when recording began, MediaPipe reported no hand landmarks, and the recorder filled all 126 values with zeros instead of leaving them undefined.

\section{After Normalisation}

The same five landmarks after dual-anchor normalisation, now expressed relative to the shoulder midpoint and scaled by shoulder width:

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Normalised pose landmarks 0 to 4, first frame}
    \label{tab:normvalues}
    \begin{tabular}{cccc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Landmark} & $x$ & $y$ & $z$ \\
        \midrule
        0 & $-$0.0054 & $-$0.7148 & $-$1.6152 \\
        1 & 0.0463 & $-$0.8491 & $-$1.4370 \\
        2 & 0.0772 & $-$0.8449 & $-$1.4374 \\
        3 & 0.1025 & $-$0.8396 & $-$1.4374 \\
        4 & $-$0.0617 & $-$0.8449 & $-$1.4758 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent The $x$ coordinates now sit around zero instead of around 0.37, because the origin has moved to the midpoint of the shoulders. The zero hand blocks remain exactly zero, since both normalisation formulas map a zero input to a zero output.

\section{After Length Standardisation}

The clip has 42 frames, fewer than the sequence length of 146, so it is zero-padded at the end. The result is a $(146, 225)$ array in which the first 42 frames are real and the remaining 104 are zero, with a boolean mask recording which is which. The final frame of the standardised clip is entirely zero, confirming that padding stays distinguishable from signal and that the masking layer will skip it.

Figure~\ref{fig:padslice} shows this same $(146, 225)$ array as a row of frames, sliced against the boolean mask computed alongside it.

% ================= Appendix E =================
\chapter{Application Interfaces}
\label{app:ui}

Two applications present a recognition result to a user. The desktop recogniser runs the Python pipeline against a webcam and was the tool used to exercise the trained model during development; the Android client is the delivered system. Both apply the decision rule of Section~\ref{sec:decisionrule}, and they differ in where the computation happens rather than in what the user is told.

\section{Desktop Recogniser}
\label{app:ui-desktop}

The desktop recogniser imports the training-time preprocessing module rather than reimplementing it, so the numbers reaching the model are the same ones the reported accuracy was measured with. That is what makes it the reference the on-device path of Section~\ref{sec:ondevice} was checked against.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{img/cant_breathe.png}
    \caption{The desktop application}
    \label{fig:ui-desktop}
\end{figure}

\noindent Recording is started and stopped from the keyboard, so one clip corresponds to one deliberate performance, as in the recorder of Appendix~\ref{app:recorder}. Displaying the top three classes rather than only the winner was deliberate: a confident wrong answer and a genuine near-tie look identical when only the winning label is shown, and the two call for different responses. It plays the same Nepali recordings the client ships, described in Section~\ref{sec:audio}, but shows no Nepali text: the image library in the pinned training environment is built without the shaping support Devanagari needs, so matras and conjuncts would be drawn in the wrong places. The client, which has a shaping engine, displays the script properly.

\section{Android Client}
\label{app:ui-client}

The client presents one of three outcomes for every attempt: an accepted phrase, which is displayed in Nepali and English and spoken from the bundled Nepali recording; an unrecognised result, when the classifier positively identifies the input as not being one of the six phrases; or a low-confidence result, when the top class is a phrase but the model will not commit to it. Only the first produces speech, and only the first displays any phrase text at all. Figure~\ref{fig:ui-client} shows two accepted results, one from each of two phrase categories.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.47\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{img/mobile_app_house_on_fire.jpeg}
        \caption{\textit{The building is on fire}, accepted at 97\%}
        \label{fig:ui-client-fire}
    \end{subfigure}
    \hfill
    \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.47\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{img/mobile_app_need_ambulance.jpeg}
        \caption{\textit{I need an ambulance}, accepted at 100\%}
        \label{fig:ui-client-ambulance}
    \end{subfigure}
    \caption{The Android client}
    \label{fig:ui-client}

\noindent The row of indicators between the camera preview and the result reports what the recogniser observed while the clip was being captured: whether pose and hands were detected, the sustained frame rate, the per-frame conversion and landmark extraction costs, the source and working frame sizes, and how many frames were dropped because a conversion was still in flight. A failure during use can therefore be attributed rather than guessed at, which is what the two distortions of Section~\ref{sec:ondevice} required to locate.

The frame counts in the banners make the temporal correction visible. The fire clip was captured as 46 frames and resampled to 125 before padding; the ambulance clip, 56 to 139. Both were captured well below the rate of the training data, and without the resampling of Section~\ref{sec:ondevice} the model would have read the shortfall as a different sign rather than a slower camera.

% ================= Appendix F =================
\chapter{Server Interface}
\label{app:protocol}

The fallback inference server exposes the endpoints in Table~\ref{tab:endpoints}. The streaming endpoint is the path the client uses when on-device recognition is switched off; the two prediction endpoints exist so that the served decision can be checked against the offline pipeline without needing a phone at all, which is what keeps this path useful as a reference even when nothing uses it in production.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Server endpoints}
    \label{tab:endpoints}
    \small
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|l|c|X|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Method} & \textbf{Path} & \textbf{Key} & \textbf{Purpose} \\
        \hline
        GET  & \texttt{/health}            & no  & Backend, class list, thresholds, target frame rate \\
        \hline
        GET  & \texttt{/classes}           & yes & Class keys and display labels \\
        \hline
        POST & \texttt{/predict/landmarks} & yes & A clip supplied as JSON, for verification \\
        \hline
        POST & \texttt{/predict/npy}       & yes & The same clip as raw array bytes \\
        \hline
        WS   & \texttt{/ws/stream}         & yes & Live frames to a decision, the path the client uses \\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\noindent The health endpoint is deliberately unauthenticated so that container health checks and the client's reachability test keep working when a key is configured. Every other endpoint requires the key, compared with a constant-time function so that a wrong key cannot be narrowed down by timing the response.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Messages on the streaming endpoint}
    \label{tab:wsmessages}
    \small
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|l|X|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Direction} & \textbf{Message} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
        \hline
        Server & \texttt{ready}     & Session established; carries the target frame rate and class list \\
        \hline
        Client & binary frame       & One JPEG frame \\
        \hline
        Server & \texttt{ack}       & Whether the frame was kept, the running frame count, and whether pose and hands were detected \\
        \hline
        Client & \texttt{done}      & Signing has finished; produce a decision \\
        \hline
        Server & \texttt{result}    & Status, phrase, confidence, and the three most probable classes \\
        \hline
        Client & \texttt{reset}     & Discard the current clip and start again \\
        \hline
        Client & \texttt{ping}      & Keepalive, sent while idle between signs \\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\noindent The on-device path carries no protocol, but it produces the same result structure (status, phrase, confidence, and the top three classes), so the presentation logic in the client is identical whichever path produced the decision. Switching between them changes where the computation happens and nothing the user sees.
